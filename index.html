How it reads

+1 — a is a square modulo p.
−1 — it is not.
0 — a = p. You win.

You may also spend a guess on 2 or −1. Neither can ever be the answer, but both are cheap information.

Where the leverage is

(−1|p) = +1 exactly when p ≡ 1 (mod 4). (2|p) = +1 exactly when p ≡ ±1 (mod 8).

For an odd prime q, reciprocity turns (q|p) into a statement about p mod 4q. Small q means few residue classes, which means a clean split of the field. Large primes answer a question almost nobody was asking.

Every ±1 is one bit, so ten guesses can separate at most 210 − 1 = 1,023 primes — fewer than the 1,228 on the board. Twelve is the real number: a player who always picks the probe with the most even split finishes every prime in twelve or fewer, and needs all twelve for 161 of them.

How it reads

The answer is the Hamming distance between x and N in ten bits. 0 means you found it. 10 means you guessed its exact complement, which is just as informative.

Why one bit at a time loses

Probing single powers of two needs 0 plus nine flips to read all ten bits — ten guesses spent, none left to submit. The board is sized so that method fails by exactly one.

Query blocks instead. For any mask M, the answer is |N| + |M| − 2·|N ∧ M|, so one guess tells you exactly how many ones of N sit inside M. Take 0 first for |N|, then halve the ten positions, then quarter them.

Each answer is one of eleven values, so the floor is ⌈log₁₁ 1024⌉ = 3. Five or six is a good round.
